Wide-area cluster catalogues from Stage-IV surveys will substantially improve the precision of cluster clustering measurements, making the control of observational systematic effects essential for cosmological inference. In preparation for the first Euclid data release (DR1), we assess the impact of the survey mask and non-homogeneous sky coverage on the two-point correlation function of galaxy clusters and its covariance. Using DR1-like lightcone mocks, we confirm that the irregular footprint does not introduce appreciable changes in the two-point correlation function when the same mask is applied to the random catalogue. We also find that spatial inhomogeneities, implemented as variations of the cluster selection function in different patches of the sky, do not significantly bias the clustering amplitude, up to levels of variation well above those expected for DR1. When the degree of inhomogeneity is increased to the regime where the effect becomes significant, we show that its amplitude is related to the maximum variation of the selection function among different regions, rather than to the number of such regions. We further find that irregular mask geometry and non-uniform sky coverage both increase the clustering covariance, introducing small, nearly scale-independent offsets that can be captured by nuisance parameters in the covariance model. Our results consolidate the treatment of mask systematic errors for DR1 and inform the methodological refinements required for future \Euclid cluster cosmology.

We demonstrate the efficacy of crowd-sourced refinement of strong gravitational lens candidate samples by citizen scientists. Binary (lens/non-lens) classification by citizen scientists has been conducted previously, and here we extend such categorisation into `definite', `probable', `possible', and `non-lenses'. This grading is a required stage ahead of any strong lens sample publication and is usually carried out by (small) teams of researchers. We compare the responses of the volunteers to those of strong lens researchers and discuss the optimal strategy for lens classification and grading in future strong lens searches. Volunteers are found to be able to correctly identify systems of each grade, and their responses can be calibrated to be comparable to those of researchers. Grade posteriors, defined as the probability that a strong lens system classified by the volunteers is a certain grade, converge most rapidly for non-lens systems. We find that one researcher classification is equivalent to only 1.9 volunteer classifications when grading such systems. There is significant disagreement in the grading of intermediate-grade lens candidates amongst both volunteers and researchers. Moving towards calibrated lens probabilities for future lens searches would help to account for such subjectivity. If the Q1 lens search was rerun with volunteers performing lens grading (and corresponding calibration), a completeness of 79% for Grades A+B (corresponding to definite + probable lenses respectively) could be achieved with 41% purity prior to researcher inspection - this would correspond to an expected 12500 true lenses (i.e. excluding contaminants) in Euclid DR1.

The Euclid mission will revolutionize our understanding of the visible Universe by collecting images and spectra of millions of galaxies across cosmic time and environments. Here, we forecast the number counts of galaxies emitting [OII], H\b{eta}, [OIII], Hα and Pa\b{eta} lines in the Euclid Wide Survey (EWS) and Deep Survey (EDS). We compare predictions from different models, including semi-analytic models and empirical scaling relations. We then simulate spectra for galaxies in the MAMBO lightcone using FastSpec, designed to rapidly generate one-dimensional spectra as observed by Euclid. We process these simulated spectra with the official Euclid spectroscopic pipeline to recover redshifts and line fluxes and derive expected counts. Predictions for Hα+[NII] emitters are broadly consistent across mocks and with existing observations. In the EWS, relative RMS scatter among mock predictions for integrated counts is approximately 19% for Hα+[NII], 44% for [OII] and 41% for [OIII], reflecting greater model dependence for oxygen-line forecasts. After accounting for flux losses and redshift misidentification, corrected flux measurements recover most intrinsic line counts, especially in the EDS. From the MAMBO-based FastSpec simulations, we predict that Euclid will detect about 42 million Hα emitters in the EWS and 1.7 million in the EDS. These non-Hα forecasts remain subject to substantial model-dependent uncertainties, as shown by scatter among the different mocks. These samples will be instrumental in pushing large-scale structure and baryon acoustic oscillation studies beyond redshift z~2, extending Euclid cosmological and galaxy evolution studies beyond what is achievable with Hα alone.

We apply FlyHash, to our knowledge for the first time, to LLM agent memory retrieval, and ask three questions a practitioner actually needs answered: does the quality advantage hold under a correctly matched-bit protocol (it does, robustly, across 10 seeds and four datasets spanning two modalities, with one specific, previously undocumented comparison pitfall identified and corrected along the way); does that advantage translate into real memory and latency savings, including against the dense-vector indexing (HNSW) practitioners default to today (no: at equal bits FlyHash's codes are larger and slower to compare than SimHash's, and HNSW dominates both hash methods at ordinary scale; a sparse-set comparison we introduce recovers most but not all of a naive latency gap, leaving hashing's practical case narrower and more specific than the quality result alone implies); and does the advantage survive genuine, non-circular agent-memory relevance labels rather than the nearest-neighbor-by-embedding proxy this literature otherwise relies on (it does). We report all three answers precisely, including the two that narrow rather than flatter the headline result, and release our implementation, a working agent-memory demo, and a full account of the methodological pitfalls we found and corrected.

50. From Handcrafted to Generative: RLVR-Optimized Icon Generation for Alexa+

Alexa+ devices with screens display animated icons during conversations to visually reinforce what is being said. The original set of these icons was hand-drawn by designers one at a time, a process that produces polished results but is time-consuming and cannot scale to the range of topics encountered in open-ended conversation. We describe an automated pipeline, deployed in production, that generates these vectorised animated icons from a text description alone and surfaces relevant animations to customers based on their ongoing conversation. The pipeline decomposes icon creation into a sequence of stages (image generation, semantic decomposition, vectorisation, styling, and animation). Its quality is determined at the very first of these, image generation, on which every downstream stage depends. We therefore focus on improving this first and most critical stage. Because the icons are rendered on small display canvases under strict design guidelines, generations are often unsuitable for production. Supervised fine-tuning is not viable, as no dataset of line-art icons exists under these constraints. Instead, we design objective verifiers that automatically flag generations violating the design guidelines, and use them to fine-tune a flow matching image generation model via reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR). The fine-tuned model raises the pass rate from 15.7% to 58.7% (3.7x) under a VLM judge that is independent of the reward model. Both the trained model and the verifier are deployed in the production pipeline, where the verifier also serves as the automated quality gate before final design review.
