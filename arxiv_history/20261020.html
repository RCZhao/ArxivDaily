1. Constraints on the Cosmic Expansion Rate Using the DECam Local Volume Exploration Survey and the Fifth Gravitational Wave Transient Catalog
Sean Patrick MacBride, Isaac McMahon, Danny Laghi, David Sánchez-Cid, Felipe Andrade-Oliveira, Marcelle Soares-Santos, Maciej Bilicki, Sarah Ferraiuolo, Surhud More, Rachel Gray, et al. Sean Patrick MacBride, Isaac McMahon, Danny Laghi, David Sánchez-Cid, Felipe Andrade-Oliveira, Marcelle Soares-Santos, Maciej Bilicki, Sarah Ferraiuolo, Surhud More, Rachel Gray, Gergely Dálya (show all)

TLDR: Applying dark siren statistical cross-matching to the non-uniform DELVE DR3 footprint yields an $H_0$ of $70.6^{+8.7}_{-7.0}\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ when combined with GW170817, demonstrating the viability of utilizing wide-area, heterogeneous galaxy surveys for gravitational-wave cosmology. (gemini)

Gravitational-wave standard sirens provide a calibration-free measure of distances, offering important input on the long-standing tension between early- and late-Universe measurements of the Hubble constant ($H_0$). In the absence of an electromagnetic counterpart, the dark siren method can be employed to statistically correlate a gravitational wave event's localization with a galaxy catalog of potential hosts. We present a measurement of the Hubble constant using the DECam Local Volume Exploration Survey Data Release 3 and the fifth Gravitational-Wave Transient Catalog. Applying the dark siren method to a large, photometrically heterogeneous footprint required revised catalog-construction choices: a faint apparent-magnitude threshold ($m_r15.5$) to exclude saturated sources, implementation of an evolving luminosity function parameterization, and de-biasing survey regions based on survey depth and photometric redshift precision. With these choices applied to DELVE, we obtain $H_0= 67.4^{+13.0}_{-11.2}\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ from dark sirens alone, and $H_0= 70.6^{+8.7}_{-7.0}\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ when combined with the bright siren GW170817. We test other galaxy catalog configuration choices, and find subdominant influence from photometric band choice and luminosity function parameterization on the final $H_0$ posterior. Our results demonstrate that extending the dark siren method to wider-area, non-uniform galaxy surveys is feasible and provides additional constraining power on $H_0$ relative to the spectral siren method, opening the door for analyses with forthcoming galaxy catalogs from LSST, Euclid, and Roman.

Optical datasets are efficient at identifying clusters and studying their properties. We leverage 13,000 $\rm deg^2$ of the sky imaged by the Dark Energy Camera (DECam) and extract over 40,000 clusters using the redMaPPer algorithm. Our catalog spans $0.2 < z < 0.9$ and $M_{\rm 200c} \gtrsim 10^{14} {\rm M}_{\odot}/h$, with four-band ($griz$) photometric redshifts that have a precision $σ_z/(1 + z) \leq 0.008$ below $z < 0.65$, and $\leq 0.011$ between $0.65 < z < 0.9$ and that exhibit an outlier rate of $f_{\rm out} < 0.2\%$. 3. Environmental dependence in the halo model informed by the FLAMINGO simulations
G. Martín Girardi, D. Rana, H. Hoekstra, J. Schaye, M. Schaller

TLDR: Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations reveal that neglecting local density dependence in halo model analyses introduces significant biases into inferred stellar-to-halo mass relations and satellite fractions during density-split galaxy-galaxy lensing studies. (gemini)

Galaxy-galaxy lensing (GGL) measurements around galaxy samples in different environments provide valuable information about the galaxy-halo connection. In such studies the halo model is commonly used to estimate physical parameters from the lensing signal, but the impact of environment is generally not considered. Our aim is to adapt the halo model to allow a density-split GGL analysis and to understand the environmental dependence of the elements of the model. We use the FLAMINGO suite of cosmological hydrodynamical simulations to construct samples of halos in underdense and overdense regions. Environment density is assigned to halos based on halo counts within an aperture, employing different aperture sizes to probe both local and large scales. We utilize spherical apertures with radii between $1 h^{-1} {\rm Mpc}$ and $10 h^{-1} {\rm Mpc}$. We find a strong dependence on environment for the halo mass function (HMF) and the halo bias. To account for this, we propose an empirical modification that can be applied to existing HMF prescriptions. We find that after a density split, the halo bias is approximately constant with mass. There is also an environmental dependence for the stellar-to-halo-mass relation (SHMR), the conditional stellar mass function, and the mass-concentration relation, all of which can be addressed with existing models. We evaluate the impact of overlooking environment on halo occupation distribution (HOD) parameter inference, finding that it leads to biased estimations of the SHMR and the satellite fraction. These results emphasize the need to review the assumptions adopted in the halo model when working with samples of galaxies binned by environment, and consequently, by galaxy properties that correlate with environment, such as morphology and color.

4. A Morphology-Based Cross-Tracer Ratio for Robust BAO Tests
Paula S. Ferreira, Carlos A. P. Bengaly, Renyue Cen

TLDR: Analyzing the cross-tracer clustering ratio between early- and late-type galaxies in DESI Legacy Survey imaging yields baryon acoustic oscillation scales consistent with standard cosmology while demonstrating that morphology-dependent acoustic responses are statistically negligible. (gemini)

We use the Cross-Tracer Clustering Ratio (CTCR) acoustic response estimator, a new probe of morphology-dependent galaxy clustering in photometric surveys. CTCR is the ratio of the auto-power spectrum of early-type galaxies (ETGs) to their cross-power spectrum with late-type galaxies (LTGs). In the matched-kernel deterministic linear-bias limit the CTCR cancels the complete projected matter spectrum, including the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) wiggles, leaving only the relative linear bias. This makes the ratio uniquely sensitive to physics beyond deterministic linear bias: any residual acoustic structure in the CTCR must arise from a difference between the normalised non-linear responses of the two tracers. We, for the first time, exploit this property to model the measured ratio as a smooth scale-dependent baseline multiplied by a scale-dependent acoustic template, and constrain the amplitude $A_{\rm morph}$ of the differential acoustic response jointly with the BAO dilation parameter $α$. Applying the estimator to nine bins of the DESI Legacy Survey Data Release 8 spanning $0.1 < z < 0.9$, we obtain$α= 0.9997 \pm 0.0383$, consistent with the Planck 2018 $Λ$CDM prediction, and $A_{\rm morph} = 0.369 \pm 0.307$, consistent with zero. A Bayesian model comparison yields at most a 2.6$σ$ preference for the acoustic template over a smooth-only baseline. The estimator is model-independent, requires no external calibration of the absolute bias, and is directly applicable to the wide-field imaging surveys that will define the next decade of cosmology. It offers a new route to testing halo occupation and assembly-bias models, and an independent, systematics-robust cross-check of BAO measurements from spectroscopic surveys.

6. Statistical Strong Lensing V. Inference with Microlensed Quasars
Shuo Zhang, Alessandro Sonnenfeld

TLDR: Hierarchically incorporating microlensing effects into flux analyses of strongly lensed quasars breaks the degeneracy between the stellar initial mass function and central dark matter distributions, significantly tightening parameter constraints on lens galaxy mass profiles. (gemini)

Microlensing of strongly lensed quasars provides direct information on the stellar component of lens galaxies, and can help break the degeneracy between the stellar initial mass function (IMF) and the dark matter. However, a statistical framework to forward model microlensing data while accounting for strong lensing selection effects is still missing. We incorporated microlensing effects into the hierarchical model of Paper III, and investigated to what extent flux measurements of strongly lensed quasars can break the degeneracy between the stellar IMF and the dark matter distribution. We tested the method on a mock sample of 219 doubly imaged quasars, lensed by circularly symmetric lenses. When microlensing effects are modelled, the inference using image fluxes recovers the input foreground population parameters and provides tighter constraints than the inference without flux constraints. The improvement is clearest in the $μ_α-μ_5$ plane, where $μ_α$ describes the mean stellar population synthesis mismatch parameter $\logα_{\rm SPS}$ and $μ_5$ describes the mean projected dark matter mass within $5\,{\rm kpc}$. The 68% credible interval on $μ_α$ shrinks from 0.16 dex to 0.09 dex, with the degeneracy between $μ_α$ and $μ_5$ significantly reduced. By contrast, fitting the image fluxes without accounting for microlensing leads to large biases on the inferred parameters. This work is a first step towards an accurate modelling of microlensing data. Application to real lens samples will require dropping the circular symmetry assumption and adopting more realistic models for the microlensing magnification.

We revisit a late-time, model-independent distance-consistency framework that combines strong gravitational lensing systems (SGLS), ultraviolet/X-ray quasars (QSOs), and 38 Sunyaev--Zel'dovich/X-ray galaxy-cluster angular-diameter distances. The analysis focuses on systematics rather than on presenting a competitive curvature measurement. Starting from the 158-system SGLS catalog, we exclude 30 systems that violate the lensing-geometric requirement $D_{A,ls}/D_{A,s}<1$ in the isothermal reference case $γ_{\rm lens}=2$, leaving a fixed 128-system SGLS parent sample for cross-matching with 1598 QSOs. TLDR: Simultaneous modeling of individual cluster weak-lensing shear profiles yields higher precision than stacked techniques and reveals that previously detected correlations between halo concentration and central galaxy magnitude gap diminish when accounting for mass variations. (gemini)

Stacking is the standard route to signal-to-noise in cluster weak lensing, but it destroys the cluster-by-cluster information that scaling-relation studies need. We instead fit every cluster's individual $ΔΣ$ profile simultaneously, tying its mass and concentration to its own richness, redshift and magnitude gap, and compare with two stacked analyses of the same data: a forward-modelled stack sharing the same model and priors, and the standard two-step analysis. The magnitude gap (the BCG-to-brightest-satellite magnitude difference) enters the concentration, which the fossil-group picture ties to earlier assembly and more relaxed systems. A gap coupling obtained in a model where the gap enters only the concentration need not survive a modest extension of that model.

9. GPU-accelerated Bayesian inference for diffracted gravitational wave signals
Zihao Lin, Jef Heynen, Mick Wright, Thomas C. K. Ng, Chris Van Den Broeck, Justin Janquart

TLDR: A GPU-accelerated Bayesian inference pipeline for gravitationally lensed gravitational waves reduces parameter estimation runtimes under wave-optics point-mass lens models from over ten hours to approximately ten minutes without sacrificing numerical accuracy. (gemini)

As the rate of gravitational wave detections continues to grow, the probability of observing a gravitationally lensed gravitational wave also increases. When the Schwarzschild radius of the lens is comparable in size to the gravitational wave wavelength, wave-optics effects induce a frequency- dependent modulation on the signal. CPU-based parameter estimation analyses for wave-optics lensing are computationally intensive, limiting the scale of studies to determine the significance of any potential lensing candidate. In this work, we implement a point-mass lens model in Jim, a high-performance GPU-based parameter estimation pipeline. This enables, for the first time, GPU-accelerated Bayesian inference in full-wave optics, including the computation of Bayes factors, without the need for training data. We call this framework Jim-lensing. Using this framework, we are able to speed up a full parameter estimation run under the point-mass lens hypothesis from more than 10 hours to the order of 10 minutes compared to the current state-of-the-art CPU-based pipeline. We show that our implementation is stable and obtains comparable results to this state-of- the-art pipeline. The substantial speed gain over existing CPU pipelines paves the way for analyzing gravitational wave data under a broader range of lensing models with greater capacity to produce robust frequentist significance estimates.

Precision weak lensing cosmology requires accurate galaxy redshift distributions. However, the spectroscopic samples used to characterize these distributions do not fully span the magnitude--color--redshift space of weak lensing source galaxies, necessitating extrapolation. This challenge will be critical at the depth of the Roman Space Telescope's High Latitude Imaging Survey (Roman HLIS). Using simulated LSST Year 4 and Roman HLIS photometry from the Cardinal simulations, we quantify how spectroscopic incompleteness biases photometric-redshift (photo-z) calibration and cosmological constraints. Propagating the estimated redshift distributions into cosmological inference, we find that existing spectroscopy is adequate for early-stage weak lensing analyses in the Roman deep fields. Conversely, full medium-tier cosmology will require further improvements in redshift characterization.

13. How accurate is Spectral Einstein Code (SpEC)? And what limits its accuracy?
Himanshu Chaudhary, Mark A. Scheel, Robert Owen, Saul A. Teukolsky, Michael Boyle, Lawrence E. Kidder, Harald P. Pfeiffer

TLDR: Investigating inspiral accuracy limits in the Spectral Einstein Code demonstrates that targeted parameter tuning mitigates constraint violations, while higher-precision gravitational waveforms will ultimately require revised formulations to overcome floating-point and multiscale errors. (gemini)

The improved sensitivity of upcoming gravitational-wave detectors will require correspondingly more accurate binary black hole (BBH) simulations. Given the high cost of such simulations, it is essential to understand their sources of error and use computational resources efficiently. We study sources of error in BBH simulations performed with the Spectral Einstein Code (SpEC) and seek to understand what limits their accuracy. In particular, we focus on the inspiral phase because it is the longest-running part of the simulation and accounts for the majority of the waveform error. Addressing these limitations will require substantial changes to both the SpEC code and the formulation it uses to evolve Einstein's equations. Many of the improvements and changes discussed in this paper are likely to be relevant to other numerical relativity codes.

16. The Local Star Formation Environment Predicts Young Star Cluster Radii: Evidence from PHANGS-MUSE H-alpha Maps
Michelle Sebastian

TLDR: Spatially resolved star formation rate surface densities on sub-galactic scales directly regulate the physical sizes of young star clusters, demonstrating that the immediate natal environment accounts for structural scatter that galaxy-integrated metrics fail to explain. (gemini)

The effective radii of young star clusters correlate with cluster mass, but the large scatter about that relation is not well understood. Theory predicts that a cluster's initial radius depends on the gas surface density of its natal environment, so the star formation environment should leave a measurable imprint on cluster structure. In a companion analysis of 5,105 clusters across 31 LEGUS galaxies, we found that galaxy-wide specific star formation rate (sSFR) carries no predictive power for cluster radius. Here we test whether that null result reflects the spatial scale on which the environment was measured. We cross-match the LEGUS cluster catalog of Brown & Gnedin with H-alpha maps from the PHANGS-MUSE survey in the four galaxies common to both surveys, and measure the local star formation rate surface density, Sigma_SFR, at the position of every cluster by aperture photometry. For the 325 clusters younger than 10 Myr with reliable radius and mass measurements, local Sigma_SFR significantly predicts effective radius (beta = 0.079, p = 2 x 10^-4) in a model containing mass and age, while the host galaxy's sSFR does not (beta = 0.042, p = 0.40) for those same clusters. The local model generalizes nearly three times better under cross-validation (CV R^2 = 0.061 versus 0.022). The effect operates among clusters within a single galaxy: it survives galaxy fixed effects (beta = 0.068, p = 0.001) and appears independently in each of the two galaxies large enough to fit alone. Including local Sigma_SFR also reduces the mass coefficient by 26%.
