Multiwavelength observations of galaxy clusters are among the most promising probes of the growth of structure and dark energy. Optically identified clusters have a high number density and can thus probe the halo mass function well into the group regime, but their weak-lensing mass calibration suffers from a selection bias induced by line-of-sight projection effects. 2. Probing Dynamical Dark Energy with Hyper Suprime-Cam Year 3 Cosmic Shear

Recent measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) and Type Ia Supernovae (SNe) have revealed a preference for a time-evolving dark energy equation of state. Because such dynamical behavior alters both the cosmic expansion history and the late-time growth of structure, weak gravitational lensing measurements can be used to test these extended models. We present an extended analysis of the real-space cosmic shear two-point correlation functions (2PCFs) from the Hyper Suprime-Cam Year 3 (HSC-Y3) survey. Using over 25 million galaxies across 416 deg$^2$, divided into four tomographic redshift bins ($0.3 < z \le 1.5$), we constrain both a constant ($w$CDM) and a time-varying ($w_0w_a$CDM) dark energy equation of state. To break the geometric degeneracies inherent in cosmic shear, we combine the HSC-Y3 data with a CMB-independent suite of background distance probes: DESI DR2 BAO, the recalibrated DES five-year SN sample (DOVEKIE), and Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) priors. In the $w$CDM paradigm, our joint analysis yields $w =-0.936^{+0.074}_{-0.067}$, consistent with a cosmological constant. Within the $w_0w_a$CDM framework, we find $w_0 =-0.832^{+0.071}_{-0.059}$ and $w_a = -1.23^{+0.66}_{-0.44}$, representing a $2.4σ$ preference for dynamical dark energy. Although the unconstrained dark energy parameters reduce the cosmic shear-only $S_8$ constraints to smaller values, our joint multi-probe analysis agrees with the baseline HSC-Y3 $Λ$CDM structure growth amplitude. We obtain $S_8 = 0.774^{+0.027}_{-0.034}$ for $w$CDM and $S_8 = 0.772^{+0.031}_{-0.030}$ for $w_0w_a$CDM. Our results demonstrate that the weak lensing amplitude is robust to extended dark energy parameterizations, and that the dynamical dark energy signal is currently driven primarily by the geometric distance ladders, a critical consistency test for next-generation Stage-IV cosmological surveys.

3. Small-scale priors for large-scale cosmology: the case of $b_φ$
Mathilde Pinon, Raul E. Angulo, Sergio Contreras, Azadeh Moradinezhad Dizgah

TLDR: We fit an extended Subhalo Abundance Matching (SHAMe) model to small-scale clustering measurements and combine it with dark-matter-only separate-universe simulations to infer a posterior on $b_φ$. (sumy fallback)

Perturbative descriptions of galaxy clustering introduce free bias parameters that capture the complex physics of galaxy formation. Local primordial non-Gaussianity (PNG), parametrized by $f_{NL}$, induces a scale-dependent contribution to the galaxy bias that is proportional to $f_{NL} b_φ$, where $b_φ$ quantifies how galaxy formation responds to PNG. Accurately determining $b_φ$ is therefore essential for robust constraints on $f_{NL}$. Current analyses usually adopt a modified universal relation, $b_φ= 2δ_c(b_1-p)$, with $p$ calibrated to simulations. This relies on the accuracy of the simulated galaxy-formation model and ignores the uncertainty on $b_φ$, potentially biasing $f_{NL}$ constraints. We investigate whether $b_φ$ can be constrained from small-scale galaxy clustering using a flexible empirical galaxy-formation model. We fit an extended Subhalo Abundance Matching (SHAMe) model to small-scale clustering measurements and combine it with dark-matter-only separate-universe simulations to infer a posterior on $b_φ$. We validate the method using IllustrisTNG hydrodynamical separate-universe simulations. Without calibration on hydrodynamical simulations, SHAMe predicts $b_φ$ with a median error of 10%, compared with 38% for a fixed halo occupation distribution (HOD) that neglects the occupation response to $φ$, and 23% for the universality relation. The residual error is dominated by central galaxies near the selection threshold and persists when using the true stellar-mass assignment conditioned on the same subhalo properties. Small-scale clustering combined with a subhalo-based empirical model predicts most of galaxy-abundance response to local PNG. The remainder depends on galaxy-formation physics and should enter $b_φ$ priors as a theoretical uncertainty. This provides a route to data-informed priors on bias parameters.

9. The Look-Elsewhere Effect in Searches for New Physics: from Classical Methods to Machine Learning
Gaia Grosso, Sergio Rodriguez-Benitez, Louis Lyons

TLDR: We highlight open problems, including the computational cost of empirical calibration for complex models and the development of analytical shortcuts for ML test statistics, and offer recommendations for reporting discovery significance in both classical and ML-based searches in particle physics. (sumy fallback)

The look-elsewhere effect (LEE) arises when the location of an effect in parameter space is not specified in advance: scanning many locations inflates the significance of the largest excess, and the local $p$-value must be corrected into a global one. A well-established framework, rooted in the work of Davies and operationalised for particle physics by Gross and Vitells, provides this correction for scans over continuous parameters. Some aspects, however, remain unsettled. The definition of "elsewhere" is ambiguous and ranges from a single distribution to an entire experimental programme. Moreover, the analytical shortcuts of the classical framework do not carry over to searches based on machine learning (ML), which are affected by an implicit post-selection bias that depends on the model's resolution and expressiveness. This review covers the LEE in searches for new physics at colliders, from its statistical foundations to these modern challenges. We discuss the corrections for continuous scans and for multiple discrete tests, illustrated with simple numerical examples, and the interaction between ML and the LEE, which we connect to the broader literature on selective inference. We highlight open problems, including the computational cost of empirical calibration for complex models and the development of analytical shortcuts for ML test statistics, and offer recommendations for reporting discovery significance in both classical and ML-based searches in particle physics.
