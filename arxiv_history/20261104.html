Carbon radio recombination lines (CRRLs) arising from the cold neutral medium (CNM) of the interstellar medium are potential foregrounds in the 1-50 MHz band that LuSEE-Night will observe from the lunar far side. Fast radio bursts (FRBs) are an emerging precision probe of cosmological fluctuations. The amount and quality of observed FRB data will grow enormously over the next decade. FRB dispersion data directly probe the free electron distribution up to intergalactic scales. Precision models of the free electron distribution are necessary to exploit this exciting new probe of large-scale structure. We show that the free electron distribution almost exactly follows the total matter distribution. We confirm this fact by estimating the electron field-level bias parameters for the first time from the FLAMINGO simulations. With simulation-based knowledge of a handful of electron free parameters, the electron distribution largely acts as a second realization of the matter distribution. Working on large scales, we can leverage this fact to combine FRB correlations with those of Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) weak lensing to constrain cosmological parameters. Assuming access to localized FRBs with redshifts, we perform cosmological forecasts using these two large-scale matter probes, which have no strong dependence on baryonic feedback models nor on parameters that encode galaxy formation. We show that the parameters $σ_8$ and $Ω_m$ are constrained at a level similar to or better than that obtained by combining FRB dispersion with weak lensing of galaxy shapes under conservative assumptions. These results motivate the use of FRB data as a complementary probe of cosmic structure and will be especially compelling as redshifts become available for localized FRB host galaxies.

4. Stress-Testing Small-Scale Cosmological Inference With Empirical Galaxy--Halo Connection Models
Hanyue Wang, Kate Storey-Fisher, Philip Mansfield, Andrew Hearin, Marcelo Alvarez, Suchetha Cooray, Risa H. Wechsler

Small-scale galaxy clustering contains substantial cosmological information, but extracting it reliably requires robust modeling of the galaxy--halo connection. In this work, we compare two widely adopted families of empirical models, subhalo abundance matching (SHAM) and the halo occupation distribution (HOD), testing their predictions for galaxy clustering and their recovery of the input cosmology. Using Aemulus-$β$, a new multi-cosmology simulation suite with substructure merger trees, we fit multiple SHAM and HOD models to the small-scale clustering of a stellar-mass-selected mock galaxy catalog generated with the more physical semi-empirical model UniverseMachine. We then use cosmological evidence modeling to infer the structure growth parameter $S_8$. Our results show that the SHAM model, regardless of the treatment of numerically disrupted subhalos, is able to offer tight, unbiased constraints on $S_8$ with ~2-3% precision, using simulations of relatively small volumes that span a limited number of cosmologies. In contrast, HOD models, even when accounting for assembly bias, yield weaker and potentially biased cosmological constraints that are sensitive to implementation choices: the inferred $S_8$ degrades in precision to ~6-11% and can be offset by ~1$σ$. This suggests that the excessive parametric flexibility and model misspecification of most commonly adopted HOD models can degrade and bias the resulting cosmological constraints. We further evaluate each model's performance in predicting higher-order statistics not used in the fit and find that SHAM generally reproduces them more accurately than the tested HOD models. These results highlight the potential of leveraging predictive subhalo-level information from simulations to enable robust cosmological inference on small scales.

Our results show the potential of DALI-based approximations to provide accurate and computationally efficient tools for forecasting in bright siren cosmology.

Ionizing radiation from quasars reduces the abundance of neutral hydrogen in the surrounding intergalactic medium (IGM), suppressing Ly$α$ absorption. This absorption deficit, known as the quasar proximity effect, can be used to measure the ultraviolet background (UVBG). We measure the hydrogen photoionization rate of the UVBG in Data Release 1 of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI). Our sample contains 6,245 quasars over $2.75\leq z<3.25$, selected with rest-frame absolute magnitudes at 1450 Angstroms of $M_{1450}\leq-25.5$ and signal-to-noise ratios of at least 3 measured redward of Ly$α$. To validate our method, we construct mock spectra matched to the luminosity and noise properties of our DESI sample using the Astrid hydrodynamical simulation at $z=3$. We also estimate a systematic uncertainty of $^{+0.837}_{-0.521}\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$.
