TLDR: Measurements of nearly five million DESI DR1 spectroscopic targets show that the cosmic homogeneity scale decreases monotonically with redshift in accordance with standard cosmological predictions, supplying highly precise empirical support for the cosmological principle up to redshift 2.1. (gemini)

We present measurements of the scale of cosmic homogeneity using the first data release (DR1) of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), the largest publicly available spectroscopic extragalactic survey to date. Using a counts-in-spheres estimator applied to nearly 5 million galaxies and quasars across six independent redshift bins spanning $0.1 < z < 2.1$, we measure the correlation dimension $D_2(r)$ and define the homogeneity scale $R_H$ as the point at which the galaxy distribution reaches within 1\% of the homogeneous asymptotic value. Our matter homogeneity scale measurements are consistent with, and substantially more precise than, previous constraints from WiggleZ and SDSS, demonstrating the power of DESI data for precision tests of the cosmological principle.

2. An Empirically Calibrated [C II] Luminosity-Halo Mass Relation for Line Intensity Mapping
Oliver Jeong, Martin White

TLDR: An empirical calibration of the high-redshift [C II] luminosity-halo mass relation indicates that intensity mapping power spectra at redshift greater than four are dominated by spatial clustering over shot noise, driven by the host halos of typical star-forming galaxies. (gemini)

Millimeter-wave line intensity mapping aims to observe the high-redshift universe through the 158 $μ$m fine-structure line of [C II], the brightest far-infrared line of star-forming galaxies. Whether a [C II] experiment at $z>4$ measures the clustering of large-scale structure, which carries rich cosmological information, rather than the shot noise of the emitters depends on the amplitude and the shape of the [C II] power spectrum. Forecasts often build the power spectrum from a halo mass--luminosity relation, $L(M_h)$, chained from scaling relations measured in other regimes or read off simulations, without a quantified uncertainty. We instead calibrate a double power-law $L(M_h,z)$ on [C II] data alone: the mean [C II] intensity from tomographic cross-correlation at $0.4<z<4.2$ and the luminosity-function measurements and limits at $z\simeq0$ and $4.3\lesssim z\lesssim8$. The four parameters of the double power law are fitted at $z=4.5$ and $5.5$ separately, with no assumption about their evolution, and then jointly across $0<z<7$, and we examine how the uncertainty depends on the modeling assumptions. The shape of the power spectrum is settled by the existing data. The clustering peak stands a factor of $45\,(23$-$93)$ and $64\,(38$-$112)$ above the shot-noise floor at $z=4.5$ and $5.5$, and the two cross at $k_c\simeq0.37$ and $0.44\,h$Mpc$^{-1}$. The amplitude is known less well, the clustering power to $0.5-0.6$ dex and the shot noise to $0.7-0.9$ dex. The results show that the signal comes from halos accessible by current galaxy surveys, those that host the Lyman-break galaxies and Ly$α$ emitters at these redshifts. The luminosity-weighted bias is relatively high, with values of $4-5$.

5. Field-level inference under pressure: the information content of the thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich field

TLDR: Applying simulation-based inference to thermal Sunyaev-Zel'dovich maps demonstrates that combining Minkowski functionals with the angular power spectrum matches the constraining power of a convolutional neural network when realistic observational noise is included. (gemini)

The thermal Sunyaev--Zel'dovich (tSZ) field is highly non-Gaussian and contains cosmological information that cannot be fully captured by its angular power spectrum. In this work, we investigate the information content of this field using a convolutional neural network (CNN), and compare to simple and interpretable summary statistics, in a simulation-based inference (SBI) framework. We perform the first field-level analysis of the tSZ field, using a suite of $10{,}000$ simulated Compton-$y$ maps, where we jointly vary the cosmological parameters $Ω_c$ and $σ_8$ (the density fraction for cold dark matter and the matter clustering amplitude, respectively) and two parameters describing the amplitude and shape of the electron pressure profile, $P_0$ and $β$, that aim to capture astrophysical uncertainties. We consider both noiseless simulations and simulations including realistic Simons Observatory (SO) noise after component separation. This is the first application of SBI to the MFs of the tSZ field. We find that the CNN provides tighter constraints than conventional summary statistics in the noiseless case. However, at SO-like noise levels, the combination of MFs and the power spectrum recovers constraints comparable to those obtained with the CNN. Our results highlight the potential of these interpretable summary statistics, as well as field-level inference, for extracting information from the tSZ field, motivating efforts toward their application to real data.

6. The Redshift-Space Cluster-Galaxy Cross-Correlation as a Probe of Gravity on Cosmic Scales

Testing General Relativity (GR) on cosmological scales requires probes that are directly sensitive to gravitational dynamics. We present a Fisher matrix forecast of how well the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Bright Galaxy Survey (BGS) can constrain departures from GR through the cluster infall velocity field, using the redshift-space cluster-galaxy cross-correlation function $ξ^{\rm s}_{\rm cg}(r_p, r_π)$ as the primary observable. We construct mock catalogs from the AbacusSummit N-body simulations, using a Halo Occupation Distribution (HOD) model for galaxies and a log-normal mass-observable relation for cluster selection. These results demonstrate the potential of DESI BGS cluster-galaxy correlations to test gravity on quasi-linear scales, complementary to existing modified gravity probes and to weak-lensing cluster mass profiles.

7. Disentangling constraints on $S_8$ from the high and low redshift CMB

TLDR: Disentangling gravitational lensing from primordial cosmic microwave background measurements lowers the inferred matter clustering parameter, effectively reconciling high-redshift cosmological data with low-redshift weak lensing measurements. (gemini)

The clustering of matter in the universe is quantified by $σ_8$, the RMS fluctuations today in linear theory in spheres of radius $8h^{-1}$ Mpc, where $h$ parametrizes the Hubble constant. Surveys of gravitational lensing and galaxy clustering best constrain $S_8\equiv (Ω_m/0.3)^{0.5} σ_8$, where $Ω_m$ is the energy density of matter in units of the critical density. Comparing this late time measurement to the value of $S_8$ inferred from the cosmic microwave background (CMB), one can test the $Λ$CDM prediction for the growth of clustering from the primordial universe to now. Several analyses report hints that CMB measurements of $S_8$ are larger than those from galaxy surveys. However, CMB measurements have information from both high and low redshift due to lensing, and these tests traditionally conflate the two. Here, we present a technique to extract constraints on the cosmological parameters from each redshift component separately, enabling a high-z vs. low-z clustering comparison that was not previously possible. We marginalize over lensing information using a principal component analysis, and show that the primordial CMB from combined Planck 2018, ACT DR6, and SPT-3G D1 prefers $S_8= 0.795\pm0.022$, in agreement with the recent determination from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) of $S_8=0.789\pm0.012$. The clustering inferred from CMB lensing is larger. However, both the level of excess lensing and increase in agreement with DES depends on the prior on the sum of neutrino masses, $\sum m_ν$. We find that removing lensing brings the CMB towards agreement with DES in any case.

9. Extracting non-Gaussian information of line-intensity maps with weighted skew spectra
Eleonora Vanzan, Azadeh Moradinezhad Dizgah, Ely D. Kovetz

TLDR: Compressing line-intensity bispectra into four optimally weighted skew spectra recovers nearly all cosmological information and yields robust measurements of non-linear galaxy bias evolution independent of the unknown mean emission intensity. (gemini)

The bispectrum of line-intensity maps carries cosmological and astrophysical information beyond the power spectrum, but its large data vector hampers the analysis of survey data. Skew spectra address this by compressing the bispectrum into a handful of power-spectrum-like observables, cross-spectra of the observed field with filtered squares of it. We present the first application of optimally weighted skew spectra to line-intensity mapping of galaxy-hosted emission, where all clustering and shot-noise terms are luminosity weighted and the mean intensity is unknown. We perform idealized real-space Fisher forecasts for a SPHEREx-like survey of H$α$ emission at $0.2 \lesssim z \lesssim 4.6$, in its deep fields and in a noisier all-sky configuration. First, the compression carries over to intensity maps: on perturbative scales and for a fixed redshift evolution of the emitters, four skew spectra, one of them matched to the shot noise, recover the bispectrum constraints on biases, shot noise and cosmological parameters to within a few percent. Second, the joint analysis of the power spectrum and the skew spectra measures the ratios of the second-order biases to the linear bias in each redshift bin. Within a bin the biases are degenerate with the unknown mean intensity, but their ratios are not, so their redshift evolution is measured without a model for the emitters. For emission that depends on halo mass alone, these ratios are determined by luminosity-weighted averages of the halo biases, so their evolution can test the halo-emission connection, including dependence on assembly history or environment. The bias ratios are not accessible to the tree-level real-space power spectrum alone. At $z \lesssim 3$ an all-sky SPHEREx-like configuration can detect departures of order $0.1$ in the bias ratios at $3σ$, while deeper fields perform better in the highest-redshift bins.

Whether models trained on different modalities converge to the same representation of the world is central to AI.
